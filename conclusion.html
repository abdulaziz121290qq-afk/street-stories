Part D · Conclusion & Engagement

What the street taught us

A summary of our key findings and our reflections on studying food, language and identity through the smallest kitchens in the city.

✎Prepared by Behroz, Saidakbar & Yakub
On this page

Key findings

We began with a simple question: what happens to cultural identity when tradition meets globalization on the street? After comparing Uzbekistan, Thailand, Vietnam and China, we reached five conclusions.

1

Street food is public identity

Because it is cooked, named and eaten in the open, street food shows who belongs to a place more clearly than restaurant food. Eating it is a daily act of belonging (Fischler, 1988).

2

Tradition was always mixed

Pad thai joined Chinese noodles to Thai nationalism (Greeley, 2009). Bánh mì turned French bread Vietnamese (Peters, 2012). Somsa shares its name with the samosa. Today’s tradition is yesterday’s exchange.

3

Names carry history

Dish names, scripts and menus record trade, empire and migration. Reading a food sign is a way of reading a society.

4

Globalization adds more than it removes

We found mixture rather than replacement, which supports Robertson (1995) over Ritzer (1993). Global chains and local stalls share the same streets and often the same customers.

5

The real risk is losing the vendor

In Bangkok, Hanoi and Chinese cities, the main threat to street food has been the removal of vendors from public space, not foreign food (Boonjubun, 2017; Hanser, 2016). Heritage status (UNESCO, 2016) and international praise do not protect the people who cook.

Answering our question

Tradition and globalization are not enemies. Street food has always absorbed outside influences and made them local. Cultural identity survives because people keep cooking, naming and sharing food in their own way. It weakens when that everyday practice is pushed out of sight, or when a dish is turned into a brand without the community behind it.

A food culture stays alive as long as someone is still allowed to cook it on the corner.Team conclusion

Reflections on the process

Working as a team of thirteen taught us as much as the reading did. We divided the project into parts, set deadlines and reviewed each other’s pages. Three things stood out.

  • We saw our own city differently. The bread stall and the somsa oven that we pass every day became evidence. Course theory gave us words for things we already knew from experience.
  • Comparison removed easy pride. Learning that famous “national” dishes were invented or borrowed made us more careful about claims that any food is pure or belongs to one nation only.
  • We became more aware as eaters. Heldke’s (2003) warning about treating other people’s food as an adventure made us think about how we behave as tourists and as customers.

Limits and future research

Our project is based on published sources and personal observation. We did not conduct interviews or surveys. Future work could record vendor oral histories in Tashkent, document the languages on bazaar signs and study how students choose between traditional and global food. These ideas are developed in Public Engagement.
