Research · Chapter 05 of 07

What scholars have missed

After reading the literature, we found four areas where research on street food is thin. These gaps explain why our comparison includes a country that is rarely studied.

✎Prepared by Abdurahmon
On this page

What the literature covers well

Scholarly work on street food has two strong traditions. The first treats it as livelihood and urban policy: who the vendors are, how much they earn and how cities regulate them (Tinker, 1997; Turner & Schoenberger, 2012; Hanser, 2016; Boonjubun, 2017; Kim, 2015). The second treats it as tourism and branding: how governments market food to visitors (Suntikul, 2019). The general anthropology of food, reviewed by Mintz and Du Bois (2002), gives the theoretical base on identity.

Gap 1: Central Asia is almost invisible

Most English-language studies of Asian street food concern Southeast and East Asia. For Uzbekistan we found general works on cuisine (Mack & Surina, 2005) and on food and politics (Rosenberger, 2012), but very little that studies bazaar and street food as a system in the way Yasmeen (2006) did for Bangkok. The somsa seller, the bread stall and the shashlik grill have not received the attention given to the Thai noodle cart.

Gap 2: language is treated as background

Studies of vendors rarely analyse the words they use. Research on the language of food focuses on restaurant menus and food writing (Jurafsky, 2014), and linguistic landscape research focuses on shop signs in general (Landry & Bourhis, 1997). Few studies bring the two together to ask how a street vendor’s sign, call or menu constructs identity, for example when a Tashkent stall writes in Uzbek, Russian and English at once.

Gap 3: comparison across regions

Most work is a single-city case study. Comparisons usually stay within one region. We found no study that places a Central Asian case next to Southeast and East Asian ones, even though the regions were connected by the same trade routes and now face similar pressures from tourism and urban “modernisation”.

Gap 4: young consumers and social media

The main books in this field were researched before short-video platforms and delivery apps changed how young people find food. How students choose between a traditional stall and a global chain, and how they talk about that choice online, is still an open question.

GapWhat existsWhat is missing
RegionDetailed studies of Bangkok, Hanoi and Chinese citiesSystematic study of street and bazaar food in Uzbekistan

GenerationResearch on vendors and touristsYoung local consumers in the age of apps and video

How our project responds

We cannot fill these gaps with a student project, but we can point in the right direction. Our website places Uzbekistan in the same frame as three well-studied countries, pays attention to dish names and scripts throughout, and draws on our own experience as young consumers in Tashkent. The limits are clear: we rely on published sources and personal observation, not on fieldwork or interviews.

In one sentenceThe literature knows a lot about who sells street food and how cities control it, but much less about what street food says, especially in Central Asia.
