How the world adapts the street

Street foods travel through migration, tourism, government campaigns and media. This chapter follows our dishes abroad and looks at what arrives in their place.

✎Prepared by Zulayho
On this page

Gastrodiplomacy: cooking as foreign policy

Thailand is the best-known example of a state using food to build its image. In 2002 the government launched the “Global Thai” programme to increase the number of Thai restaurants abroad, a strategy that Rockower (2012) calls gastrodiplomacy: winning hearts and minds through stomachs. Suntikul (2019) shows how this links to tourism, since people who enjoy a cuisine abroad are more likely to visit the country. Pad thai, once promoted to Thai citizens as a national dish (Greeley, 2009), became the dish that represents Thailand to foreigners.

Migration: the dish follows the people

Vietnamese food spread mainly through migration. Communities that settled in North America, France and Australia after 1975 opened small bakeries and noodle shops, and bánh mì and phở slowly entered mainstream menus. The entry of “banh mi” into the Oxford English Dictionary in 2011 marks the point at which a Vietnamese word no longer needed translation (People’s Army Newspaper, 2011).

Abroad, recipes change. Portions grow, chilli is reduced, fillings follow local taste. Whether this is loss or creativity depends on the point of view, which leads to the debate on authenticity.

Authenticity and the “food adventurer”

Heldke (2003) criticises the attitude of the “food adventurer” who collects exotic cuisines as experiences and expects other people’s food to stay cheap and unchanged. Johnston and Baumann (2010) find that authenticity and exoticism are the two main ways in which food writers give value to a dish. Both studies warn that praise from outside can freeze a cuisine in place and ignore the people who cook it.

The opposite direction: global food arrives

Globalization also brings foreign food in. Ritzer (1993) expected standardisation, but the research collected by Watson (1997) found that customers in East Asia changed the meaning of fast food. In Beijing, McDonald’s became a place for long social visits and a symbol of modern life rather than quick eating (Yan, 1997). This is glocalization in practice (Robertson, 1995).

We see similar mixing in Tashkent, where international chains opened only in the late 2010s. Local fast-food brands sell lavash wraps and burgers next to somsa bakeries, and young people move easily between both. The global did not replace the local; the two now share one street.

Heritage lists and stars

International institutions now rank and protect street-level food. UNESCO’s recognition of palov culture (UNESCO, 2016), Singapore’s promotion of hawker centres as tourist attractions (Henderson et al., 2012) and a Michelin star for a Bangkok street cook (Agence France-Presse, 2017) all turn everyday eating into official culture. Wilk (1999) observed the same effect in Belize: a “national cuisine” often becomes clear to local people only after outsiders and tourists ask for it.

FindingGlobal reception is uneven. Countries with state campaigns or large diasporas have world-famous street foods. Uzbekistan’s equally rich street food is still largely unknown outside its region, which is one of the gaps we discuss in the next chapter.
