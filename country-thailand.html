Thailand: a nation built on a noodle

Bangkok is often called the street-food capital of the world. Its most famous dish was promoted by the state, and its vendors are both national symbols and targets of city clean-ups.

✎Prepared by Research team
On this page

The scene

In Bangkok, buying prepared food outside the home is ordinary life. Office workers queue at carts for breakfast, families carry dinner home in bags and night markets fill the pavements after dark. Yasmeen (2006) calls this the city’s “foodscape” of public eating. Thompson (2010) documents hundreds of dishes that belong to the street rather than to the home kitchen or the restaurant.

Signature street foods

ผัดไทย

Pad Thai

Stir-fried rice noodles with egg, tofu, dried shrimp, bean sprouts and peanuts, balanced between sweet, sour and salty.

ส้มตำ

Som Tam

Green papaya salad pounded in a mortar with lime, chilli, fish sauce and palm sugar. It comes from the north-east (Isan).

หมูปิ้ง

Moo Ping

Grilled pork skewers sold with a small bag of sticky rice, the classic breakfast eaten on the way to work.

ข้าวเหนียวมะม่วง

Mango Sticky Rice

Sweet coconut sticky rice with ripe mango, a seasonal dessert that became a tourist favourite.

Language on the street

The full name of the national dish, kway teow pad Thai, combines a Chinese word for rice noodles with the Thai for “stir-fried Thai-style” (Greeley, 2009). The name itself records the meeting of Chinese technique and Thai identity. In tourist areas, carts carry English menus and photographs; in local lanes the same dishes are ordered with a few spoken words and no written menu at all.

Identity: a planned national dish

In the late 1930s and 1940s, Prime Minister Plaek Phibunsongkhram’s government promoted pad thai during a campaign to modernise and unify the country (Greeley, 2009). Decades later the state used food again, this time abroad: the 2002 “Global Thai” programme supported Thai restaurants overseas as a form of gastrodiplomacy (Rockower, 2012; Suntikul, 2019). Thai street food is therefore both everyday habit and deliberate national branding.

Tradition meets globalization

Global recognition reached the street in December 2017, when the first Michelin Guide Bangkok awarded a star to Jay Fai, who cooks over charcoal at a street-side shop (Agence France-Presse, 2017). Yet since 2014 the city administration has removed vendors from many pavements in the name of order (Boonjubun, 2017; Associated Press, 2017), replacing an earlier system of tolerant, informal regulation (Batréau & Bonnet, 2016).

The Thai paradoxStreet food is promoted to the world as the soul of Thailand, while the space where it is made keeps shrinking. Fame does not automatically protect the vendor.
