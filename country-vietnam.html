Vietnam: the baguette that changed sides

On low plastic stools along the pavement, Vietnam eats dishes that began with colonial ingredients and ended as national icons.

✎Prepared by Research team
On this page

The scene

In Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City the pavement is a kitchen, a shop and a dining room. Some vendors have a fixed corner; others carry everything on a shoulder pole or a bicycle. Kim (2015) shows how each stretch of sidewalk is shared by vendors, residents and motorbikes through unwritten agreements. Many mobile vendors are women who travel in from villages (Turner & Schoenberger, 2012).

Signature street foods

Phở

Pho

Rice noodles in a clear beef or chicken broth with herbs and lime. It appeared in northern Vietnam in the early twentieth century.

Bánh mì

Banh mi

A light baguette filled with pâté, grilled or cold meats, pickled carrot and daikon, coriander and chilli.

Bún chả

Bun cha

Grilled pork served in a bowl of sweet-sour dipping sauce with rice vermicelli and herbs, a Hanoi lunchtime speciality.

Cà phê sữa đá

Iced milk coffee

Strong drip coffee over sweetened condensed milk and ice, another French import made Vietnamese.

Language on the street

Vietnamese food vocabulary keeps its colonial history in plain sight. Bánh mì names a French bread with a Vietnamese word; cà phê comes from café; pa tê from pâté. The borrowing later ran the other way: “banh mi” entered the Oxford English Dictionary in 2011 (People’s Army Newspaper, 2011), and “pho” is now ordinary English. A language that once absorbed the coloniser’s words now exports its own.

Identity: making foreign food local

Peters (2012) and Vu (2016) describe how Vietnamese cooks adopted French ingredients selectively and combined them with local herbs, fish sauce and rice. The result does not taste French. Avieli (2012) shows in his study of Hội An that everyday dishes express local and national belonging in ways people rarely put into words. Bánh mì is a good example of identity made through adaptation rather than purity.

Tradition meets globalization

Economic reform after 1986 filled the streets with private food sellers. As the cities modernised, authorities began to restrict them: in 2008 Hanoi banned vending on many streets, and vendors answered with mobility and quiet resistance (Turner & Schoenberger, 2012; Eidse et al., 2016). Meanwhile Vietnamese street dishes became global restaurant trends. Vietnam shows glocalization (Robertson, 1995) twice: first when it localised French food, and again as the world localises Vietnamese food.

Key ideaBánh mì proves that a dish can have foreign origins and still be completely authentic to the people who made it their own.
