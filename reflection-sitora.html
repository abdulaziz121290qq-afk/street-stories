Sitora

Research · Part B

Coordinated Part B with Shakhnoza: collected and edited the team’s individual course reflections.

Key learnings

The readings on gender changed how I see street vendors. Tinker (1997) found that women run a large share of street-food businesses in many countries and that this income supports families. Yasmeen (2006) describes how public eating in Bangkok made life possible for working women on both sides of the counter.

In Uzbekistan I see a different pattern. Women bake and sell bread, but the public cooking of palov and shashlik is done by men, while women cook the same dishes at home without an audience. The skill is equal. The visibility is not.

Personal motto

My guiding phraseAsk who is cooking, and who gets the credit.

Food work is real work. Much of it is done by women and too much of it is invisible.

Applying course themes

I will notice and value the cooking that happens out of sight, starting in my own family. If I continue in research, I would like to interview women bread sellers in Tashkent about their work, because their voices are missing from what we read.

Sources on this page

  • Tinker, I. (1997). Street foods: Urban food and employment in developing countries. Oxford University Press.
  • Yasmeen, G. (2006). Bangkok’s foodscape: Public eating, gender relations, and urban change. White Lotus Press.
