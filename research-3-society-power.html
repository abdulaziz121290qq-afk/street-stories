Who gets to cook on the sidewalk?

Street food looks informal, but it is shaped by class, gender, ethnicity and state power. This chapter asks who sells, who eats and who decides.

✎Prepared by Zulayho
On this page

Class: cheap food, changing status

Bourdieu (1984) argued that taste is not only personal. What people like to eat marks their social position. For most of its history, street food was the food of workers, students and migrants: filling, quick and affordable. In Bangkok it remains an essential part of how low-income residents eat every day (Yasmeen, 2006).

At the same time, street food has become fashionable among middle-class “foodies” and tourists who value it as authentic. Johnston and Baumann (2010) describe this as a new form of distinction: showing good taste by knowing the “real” cheap places. The same bowl of noodles can therefore be a necessity for one customer and a cultural experience for another.

Gender: women at the wok

Across Southeast Asia, street vending is largely women’s work. Tinker’s (1997) comparative study found that women ran a large share of street food businesses and used the income to support their households. Yasmeen (2006) shows that in Bangkok small food shops let women combine paid work with family duties, and that buying prepared food freed other working women from daily cooking.

In Uzbekistan the picture is different. Public cooking of palov and shashlik is traditionally done by men, while women dominate home kitchens and many bazaar bread stalls. Comparing the cases shows that “who cooks in public” is a cultural rule, not a natural fact.

Ethnicity and migration

Street food is often the work of minorities and newcomers. Chinese migrants shaped the noodle stalls of Bangkok. Rural women who walk into Hanoi each day with shoulder poles are internal migrants with few rights in the city (Turner & Schoenberger, 2012). In Chinese cities many vendors are rural migrants as well (Hanser, 2016). The food that later becomes a national symbol frequently begins with people at the edge of the nation.

Power: cleaning the streets

City governments tend to see vendors as a problem of order, traffic and hygiene. The pattern is similar in three of our countries:

CityWhat happenedSource
BangkokAfter 2014 the city government cancelled most permitted vending zones to “return the pavements to pedestrians”. In 2017 a plan to clear stalls citywide caused international criticism.Boonjubun (2017); Associated Press (2017)
HanoiA 2008 ban removed vendors from many streets and public spaces. Vendors responded with quiet tactics: moving constantly, watching for police, relying on personal contacts.Turner & Schoenberger (2012); Eidse et al. (2016)
Chinese citiesUrban management officers (chengguan) police vendors as part of “civilised city” campaigns, sometimes leading to open conflict on the street.Hanser (2016); Bell & Loukaitou-Sideris (2014)

Before these campaigns, Bangkok was often described as a model of “managed informality”, where officials tolerated and informally regulated vendors (Batréau & Bonnet, 2016). Kim (2015) shows a similar everyday negotiation over sidewalk space in Ho Chi Minh City.

The paradox of pride and removal

In December 2017 the first Michelin Guide Bangkok gave a star to Jay Fai, a street-side cook in her seventies (Agence France-Presse, 2017). In the same year, city officials were removing ordinary vendors from the pavements. The state celebrates street food as heritage and as a tourist attraction, but treats the people who produce it as a nuisance.

Course linkPower decides whose food counts as culture. When street food is praised as “authentic” but vendors lose their place, identity is being separated from the people who created it.
