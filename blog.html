Part D · Public Blog

Join the conversation

Three short posts from our team, each ending with an open question. Read them, then share your own experience of street food, language and identity.

✎Prepared by Behroz, Saidakbar & Yakub
Post 1 · Tradition · by Behroz

Is a somsa from a café still a somsa?

New bakeries in Tashkent sell somsa with cheese, chicken or spinach, baked in electric ovens and served on a plate with a logo. Older customers sometimes say it is “not real”. But the tandir version was once a new idea too, and our research shows that dishes like pad thai and bánh mì were created through change.

Maybe the question is not which recipe is correct, but who decides. If the people who grew up with a food accept the new version, is it authentic?

💬 Discussion question: Where do you draw the line between a new version of a traditional dish and a different dish?
Post 2 · Language · by Saidakbar

Why do new food places choose English names?

Walk through any growing city in our four countries and you will see food signs in English, even where few customers speak it. In Tashkent, a place called “Street Food” may sell exactly what the bazaar sells under an Uzbek name. English seems to promise something modern and international.

Linguistic landscape research suggests that the language of a sign shows who the business wants to attract and what image it wants. The dish is the same. The identity being sold is different.

💬 Discussion question: Does the language on a sign change what you expect from the food?
Post 3 · Globalization · by Yakub

Fame without protection

In 2017 a Bangkok street cook received a Michelin star. In the same period the city was removing thousands of ordinary vendors from its pavements. Tourists come for the street food, yet the street itself is being cleared.

This made us think about our own bazaars. If Uzbek food becomes famous abroad, who will benefit: the vendors who kept the recipes alive, or new businesses built for visitors?

💬 Discussion question: How can a city welcome tourists and modernise without losing its street vendors?
Academic netiquette

Before you comment

1. Be respectful

Disagree with ideas, not with people. Food is personal and tied to family and culture, so write about other cuisines as you would like others to write about yours.

2. Support claims with evidence

If you state a fact, say where it comes from. Use APA style when you cite a source, for example (Fischler, 1988).

3. Stay on topic

Keep comments connected to food, language, identity and the questions in the posts.

4. Avoid stereotypes

Do not judge a culture by one dish or one experience. Describe what you observed and where.

5. Write clearly

Use full sentences and a polite academic tone. Avoid slang, capital letters for emphasis and sarcasm that can be misread.

6. Respect privacy

Do not share personal details or photos of vendors and other people without their permission.
