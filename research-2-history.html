Research · Chapter 02 of 07

From caravan stops to night markets

Street food is older than restaurants. This chapter traces how trade, empire, migration and state policy shaped the foods sold on the streets of our four countries.

✎Prepared by Ismoiljon
On this page

Uzbekistan: food of the Silk Road

The cities of today’s Uzbekistan grew along the trade routes that linked China, Persia, India and the Mediterranean. Bazaars fed travellers as well as residents, and many foods still sold on the street are travel foods: bread that keeps for days, meat grilled on skewers, pastries baked on the wall of a clay tandir oven (Mack & Surina, 2005). The choyxona (teahouse) was the public place for men to eat, talk and do business.

During the Soviet period, eating out was reorganised around state canteens and standard recipes, but bazaars and home cooking kept regional dishes alive. After independence in 1991, national cuisine became part of nation-building (Rosenberger, 2012). In 2016 UNESCO inscribed “Palov culture and tradition” on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (UNESCO, 2016).

Thailand: from canals to carts

In old Bangkok food was sold from boats on the canals. As roads replaced waterways, vendors moved onto the streets. Chinese migrants introduced noodles and the wok, and by the twentieth century prepared food bought outside the home was normal for city families (Van Esterik, 1992; Yasmeen, 2006).

The most famous Thai dish was partly a political project. In the late 1930s and 1940s the government of Plaek Phibunsongkhram promoted a rice-noodle dish, kway teow pad Thai, as part of a campaign to build a modern national culture. The noodles and the technique were Chinese in origin; the name declared the result Thai (Greeley, 2009).

Vietnam: the baguette becomes bánh mì

French colonial rule brought wheat bread, coffee, pâté and beef-eating habits to Vietnam. Vietnamese cooks made these their own. Phở appeared in the north in the early twentieth century, and the baguette was filled with local herbs, pickles and chilli to become bánh mì (Peters, 2012; Vu, 2016). After the 1986 Đổi Mới reforms allowed private trade, street vending expanded quickly in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City.

The word itself has travelled: “banh mi” was added to the Oxford English Dictionary in March 2011 (People’s Army Newspaper, 2011).

China: snacks, cities and reform

Chinese cities have had street snacks (xiaochi) and night markets for many centuries, and regional specialities were a source of local pride (Swislocki, 2009). Under the planned economy private vending was heavily restricted. It returned with the market reforms after 1978, at the same moment that foreign fast food arrived. McDonald’s opened in Beijing in 1992 and was quickly adapted by local customers, who used it as a place to sit and socialise rather than to eat quickly (Yan, 1997; Watson, 1997).

A shared timeline

Before 1900Trade routes and bazaars

Silk Road caravan food in Central Asia; canal boats in Bangkok; snack streets in Chinese cities.

1880s–1940sEmpire and nation

French bread and beef reach Vietnam; Thailand promotes pad thai as a national dish.

1950s–1970sState control

Soviet canteens in Uzbekistan and planned economies in China and Vietnam limit private vending.

1978–1991Markets return

Reform in China (1978), Đổi Mới in Vietnam (1986) and Uzbek independence (1991) bring vendors back.

1990s–todayGlobal stage

Fast-food chains, tourism, UNESCO lists, dictionaries and Michelin guides turn street food into heritage and brand.

Key pointNone of these “traditional” foods is pure or ancient in a simple sense. Each is the result of earlier globalizations: trade, migration, colonialism and state planning. Tradition is a process, not a starting point.
