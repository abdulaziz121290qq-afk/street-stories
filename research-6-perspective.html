Where we stand in the debate

Does globalization destroy local food culture or renew it? This chapter sets out the main academic positions and explains the view our team takes.

✎Prepared by Abdurahmon
On this page

Debate 1: sameness or mixture?

The first position is homogenisation. Ritzer (1993) argues that the logic of fast food, with its efficiency, calculability, predictability and control, spreads through societies and reduces variety. On this view, a burger chain next to a bazaar is the beginning of the end for the bazaar.

The second position is glocalization. Robertson (1995) and the researchers in Watson (1997) show that global products are reinterpreted locally and that local cultures respond by defining themselves more clearly. Wilk (1999) even argues that global contact helps to create local cuisine, because people begin to ask what “our food” is.

Debate 2: is authenticity real?

Some writers treat authenticity as a quality that food has or loses. Others see it as a label given by people with the power to judge. Appadurai (1988) showed that a “national cuisine” can be constructed, in his case by cookbooks in India. Heldke (2003) and Johnston and Baumann (2010) show that the search for authentic food often serves the status of the eater more than the interests of the cook.

Debate 3: heritage or livelihood?

Governments and international bodies increasingly protect food as heritage (UNESCO, 2016), and food is an everyday tool of nationalism (Ichijo & Ranta, 2016). Urban researchers answer that heritage policy means little if vendors are removed from the streets (Boonjubun, 2017; Hanser, 2016).

Our position

Based on our four cases, we take a position between the extremes.

1

Tradition is dynamic

Pad thai, bánh mì and even somsa are products of earlier contact between cultures. We do not treat any dish as a fixed original that globalization can only damage.

2

Glocalization, with limits

We agree with Robertson more than with Ritzer: the evidence shows mixture. But mixture is not equal. Chains have capital and legal space; vendors often have neither.

3

Authenticity belongs to people

We understand authenticity as a relationship between cooks, eaters and place, not as a list of correct ingredients. A dish is authentic when its community recognises it as theirs.

4

No heritage without vendors

Protecting a dish while removing the people who cook it protects only a brand. The real threat to street food is losing public space, not the arrival of foreign flavours.

Why identity survives

Fischler (1988) explains why people hold on to familiar food: eating is an act of trust, and known foods tell us who we are. This helps to explain what we see in Tashkent. Our generation enjoys burgers and bubble tea, yet nobody imagines a wedding without palov or a morning without fresh non. Global food is added to the menu of identity; it does not delete what was there.

Our thesisStreet food shows that tradition and globalization are not opposites. Tradition has always been made through exchange. What decides whether a food culture stays alive is whether ordinary vendors keep the right to cook, sell and name their food in public.
