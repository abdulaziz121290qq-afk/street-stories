Research · Chapter 07 of 07

From research to real projects

Research on street food should not stay on a website. This chapter proposes practical ways our findings could inform education, city policy and tourism, with a focus on Tashkent.

✎Prepared by Abdurahmon
On this page

Why go public?

Our main finding is that street food carries identity only as long as vendors can work and their knowledge is passed on. That is a practical matter, so we suggest five projects that students, schools and city institutions could realistically carry out.

🗺️

1. A street-food map of Tashkent

A student-made online map of bazaars and long-standing stalls, with the story of each vendor, the names of dishes in Uzbek, Russian and English, and a short audio of how to order. It would document knowledge that is not written anywhere.

Education · Digital heritage
🎙️

2. Vendor oral histories

Recorded interviews with bakers, somsa makers and shashlik masters about how they learned their craft. UNESCO’s recognition of palov culture stresses transmission between generations (UNESCO, 2016); an archive would support it.

Research · Community
🏫

3. A school lesson: “Read the menu”

A one-hour lesson for language classes in which pupils collect photos of food signs and discuss which languages and scripts appear and why. It applies the linguistic landscape idea (Landry & Bourhis, 1997) in a simple way.

Education · Language
🏛️

4. Policy note: space for vendors

A short brief for city planners that summarises what happened in Bangkok after vending zones were cancelled (Boonjubun, 2017) and what worked when vending was tolerated and managed (Batréau & Bonnet, 2016).

Policy · Urban planning
🧳

5. Responsible food tourism

Singapore has promoted its hawker centres to visitors while they remain everyday eating places for residents (Henderson et al., 2012), and Thailand used food to bring visitors (Suntikul, 2019). Uzbekistan’s tourism is growing, and food tours could send income directly to bazaar vendors. Guides should present vendors as skilled people, not as exotic scenery, which is the risk Heldke (2003) describes.

Tourism · Economy

Who benefits

AudienceWhat they gain
Students and teachersLocal material for teaching language, history and cultural identity
VendorsVisibility, recognition of skill and a stronger case for their place in the city (Tinker, 1997)
City authoritiesEvidence from other Asian cities before making decisions about markets and pavements
VisitorsA more respectful and informed way to experience local food

What this website already does

This site is a first step. The Street Food Atlas presents each country’s dishes with their original names and scripts, the Glossary explains course concepts in plain language, and the Discussion page invites readers to add their own experience.

A simple first actionNext time you buy from a street vendor, ask what the dish is called and where the recipe comes from. That small conversation is how food knowledge has always been passed on.
