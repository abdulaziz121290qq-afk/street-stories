Ten things we learned

The ten key findings of our project in one place. Each card links to the chapter where the evidence is presented.

✎Prepared by Behroz, Saidakbar & Yakub
01

Street food is a public language

A stall shows who cooks, who eats and what food is called. It is culture that anyone can read from the pavement.

Read the evidence →02

About 2.5 billion people eat it daily

The FAO estimate shows that street food is a major world food system, not a minor curiosity.

Read the evidence →03

“Traditional” dishes are products of exchange

Silk Road trade, Chinese migration and French colonialism created foods that now stand for national identity.

Read the evidence →04

Pad thai was promoted by the state

Thailand’s government pushed the dish in the 1930s–40s to build a modern national culture.

Read the evidence →05

Bánh mì reversed a colonial borrowing

A French baguette became Vietnamese, and in 2011 the Vietnamese word entered the Oxford English Dictionary.

Read the evidence →06

Palov is officially world heritage

UNESCO inscribed Uzbekistan’s palov culture and tradition in 2016 as a practice that binds communities.

Read the evidence →07

Who cooks in public depends on culture

Women run much of Southeast Asia’s street food; in Uzbekistan, public palov and shashlik are men’s work.

Read the evidence →08

Cities celebrate the food and remove the vendor

A Bangkok street cook won a Michelin star in 2017 while the city was clearing stalls from its pavements.

Read the evidence →09

Globalization mixes more than it replaces

Evidence from all four countries supports glocalization: chains and stalls share streets and customers.

Read the evidence →10

Central Asia is missing from the research

Uzbek street and bazaar food is rarely studied in English, and vendor language is rarely studied anywhere.
