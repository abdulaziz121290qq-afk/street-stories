What is street food, and why does it matter?

Street food is the most public meal a society has. This chapter defines our topic and explains why a paper plate of noodles belongs in a course on food, language and identity.

✎Prepared by Ismoiljon
On this page

Defining street food

Street food means ready-to-eat food and drink that is prepared and sold in streets, markets and other public places, usually by small independent vendors working from carts, stalls or a piece of pavement (Tinker, 1997). It is cheap, fast and cooked in front of the customer. The Food and Agriculture Organization has estimated that about 2.5 billion people eat street food every day (FAO, 2007), which makes it one of the largest food systems on the planet and one of the least studied.

Our project looks at four places where street food is part of everyday life: Uzbekistan, Thailand, Vietnam and China. Thailand and Vietnam are the classic Southeast Asian cases in the literature. China is the source of many techniques that travelled south. Uzbekistan is our own home, a Silk Road food culture that is almost absent from English-language research.

Why food is never only food

Anthropologists have long argued that eating is a way of communicating. Mary Douglas (1972) described a meal as a code: what is served, in what order and with whom tells us about social boundaries. Claude Fischler (1988) added that because food is literally taken into the body, people feel that they become what they eat. This is why food is so closely tied to identity. In their review of the field, Mintz and Du Bois (2002) show that food studies now covers memory, ethnicity, class and nation, not only nutrition.

Street food makes these ideas visible. A restaurant hides its kitchen and its customers behind walls. A street stall does the opposite. Anyone walking past can see who cooks, who eats, how much it costs and which language is used to order.

Tell me what you eat, and I will tell you what you are.Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin, The Physiology of Taste (1825)

The language on the cart

The names of dishes are small pieces of history. Bánh mì is simply the Vietnamese word for bread, yet the bread is a French baguette. Pad thai means “Thai-style stir-fry”, a name that had to state its nationality because the technique was Chinese. Somsa shares a root with the Indian samosa, a trace of trade routes across Asia. As Jurafsky (2014) shows, following a food word across languages often reveals who traded with, ruled over or migrated to whom.

Signs and menus matter too. Researchers call the visible written language of a public space its linguistic landscape (Landry & Bourhis, 1997). A Tashkent bazaar sign in Uzbek Latin script, Cyrillic and English, or a Bangkok cart with a hand-written English menu for tourists, shows which audiences a vendor is speaking to.

Tradition meets globalization

Two well-known theories frame our title. Ritzer (1993) warned of McDonaldization: the spread of fast, standardised, predictable food that could make every city taste the same. Robertson (1995) answered with glocalization: global forms are always adapted locally, so the result is mixture rather than sameness. Street food is a good test of both ideas, because it is local by nature but now sits beside global chains, appears in tourist guides and travels abroad with migrants.

Our research questionHow do street foods in Uzbekistan, Thailand, Vietnam and China carry cultural identity through taste and language, and what happens to that identity when globalization arrives?

Relevance to the course

  • Foodways: street food is a complete system of producing, selling and sharing food in public.
  • Language: dish names, vendor calls and menus show borrowing, translation and code-switching.
  • Identity and power: governments promote some street foods as national symbols while removing the vendors who make them.
  • Globalization: the same dish can be heritage at home, a trend abroad and a product in a franchise.
