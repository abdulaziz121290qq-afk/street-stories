Part A · Introduction

About Street Stories

A team project for ILC 2250 Food, Language and Identity at Webster University in Tashkent. Thirteen students, four countries and one question about what street food tells us.

✎Prepared by Kamola
On this page

Our theme

Street Food and Cultural Identity: Tradition Meets Globalization. We study the food sold on streets and in bazaars because it is where culture is most public. A stall shows what a community eats, how it names its food and who is allowed to sell it. Following Fischler (1988), we treat food as a way in which people define who they are.

Our title contains a tension. Tradition suggests continuity; globalization suggests change. Some scholars expect global fast food to make cities the same (Ritzer, 1993). Others show that global and local mix into new forms (Robertson, 1995). We test these ideas against four real food cultures.

Why these four countries

🫓

Uzbekistan

Our home and our lived experience. A Silk Road food culture that is rarely included in research on Asian street food.

 🍜

Thailand

The best-known street-food culture in Southeast Asia, where a national dish was shaped by government policy.

 🥖

Vietnam

A case of colonial ingredients turned into national icons, and of a food word that entered English.

 🥟

China

The source of noodles and stir-frying across the region, now balancing snack traditions with rapid urban change.

What we wanted to find out

  1. How do street foods express cultural identity through taste, names and ways of selling?
  2. How have trade, colonialism, migration and state policy shaped foods that are now called traditional?
  3. What happens to these foods, and to the people who make them, under globalization?
  4. What can a comparison with Southeast and East Asia tell us about Uzbekistan?

How we worked

This is a literature-based project. We read academic books and journal articles on food, identity and street vending (Tinker, 1997, among many others), checked facts against institutional sources such as UNESCO and FAO, and added our own observations as residents of Tashkent. All sources are cited in APA style and listed on the References page.

How to read this siteStart with the Research Knowledge Base for the full argument, explore the Street Food Atlas country by country, then read each member’s personal reflection. The Conclusion and 10 Key Insights summarise what we found.
